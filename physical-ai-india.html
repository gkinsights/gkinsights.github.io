Robots in a low-wage economy: Where physical AI is likely to pay off in India

McKinsey benchmarks humanoid robots against the cost of human labor, and in India that labor costs a fraction of what it does in the markets where humanoids are being piloted. This analysis argues that physical AI in India is therefore more likely to be justified by quality and export requirements than by headcount savings.

by Gaurav Kumar · October 2026

Physical AI, here meaning AI systems combined with machines that sense and act in the physical world, has moved from research labs to boardroom agendas. McKinsey describes it as technologies that can sense and interact with the real world, with humanoid robots and autonomous vehicles as headline applications, and identifies cost improvements, compute breakthroughs, and at-scale deployment as its drivers. The International Federation of Robotics (IFR) reports that industrial robot installations exceeded 600,000 units worldwide in 2025.

Much of the economic case for robots is framed as a comparison with the cost of human labor. That comparison works very differently in India. This analysis argues that physical AI will matter in India, but that adoption is likely to follow quality, consistency, and export requirements more than wage savings. That is an interpretation of the evidence below rather than a settled finding.

India is a growing but small market for industrial robots

India installed almost 10,500 industrial robots in 2025, up 15 percent, and has grown at an average of 27 percent a year since 2020, according to the IFR. It ranks sixth in the world for new installations. The scale gap with the leaders remains large (Exhibit 1).

The most recent detailed breakdown for India, for 2024, showed demand concentrated in the automotive industry, which took 45 percent of installations. Within automotive, installations by parts suppliers grew 40 percent while those by car manufacturers fell 3 percent. In 2024, at least, robots in India were concentrated in a few industries rather than spread across manufacturing.

The labor-substitution case is weaker in India than in the markets piloting humanoids

The economics of a robot depend partly on what it replaces. Wage levels illustrate the gap. On a purchasing-power-adjusted basis, India's monthly minimum wage was about $233 in 2024, against $544 in China, $1,257 in the United States, and $1,839 in Japan, according to ILO data compiled by Visual Capitalist and reported by Business Today. Minimum wages are only a proxy for factory labor costs, and because robots are priced in dollars, the gap at market exchange rates is wider still. The order of magnitude matters: a machine that pays back by replacing labor in Japan or the United States faces a much harder comparison in India.

Humanoid robots make the point sharply. McKinsey estimates that current humanoid prototypes cost $150,000 to $500,000 per unit, and that costs would need to fall to roughly $20,000 to $50,000 for humanoids to compete with human labor across mainstream sectors. McKinsey also notes that humanoid battery life of two to four hours falls well short of typical eight- to 12-hour shifts. McKinsey does not estimate payback by country. This analysis expects, given the wage gap, that general-purpose humanoids would need to fall well below McKinsey's range before labor substitution alone justifies them in India.

Quality, consistency, and export requirements offer a stronger case

The stronger case for physical AI in India is likely to rest on output quality rather than fewer workers. Three conditions are likely to support adoption regardless of labor cost.

Precision and consistency requirements. Processes such as welding, painting, inspection, and fine assembly reward repeatability. Automotive parts suppliers, whose installations grew fastest within India's automotive sector in 2024, serve customers with demanding quality standards, which may help explain that growth.

Export-oriented production. India's electronics exports reached 3.27 lakh crore rupees in FY25, including 2 lakh crore rupees of mobile phones, and electronics exports reached $22.2 billion in the first half of FY26 alone, according to government data. Lines that supply global brands are, in this analysis, the most likely places for automation to be driven by customer requirements rather than by the factory's own wage math.

Hazardous and repetitive handling. Among the early humanoid use cases McKinsey identifies are inspection at hazardous sites and repetitive tote movement in warehouses, applications where safety and consistency matter as much as cost.

Industrial policy emphasizes jobs, and automation reduces labor per unit

Government reporting on India's production-linked incentive (PLI) scheme for large-scale electronics manufacturing highlights employment alongside output: more than 1.35 lakh direct jobs and 8.56 lakh crore rupees of cumulative production through June 2025. In government reporting on the scheme, employment is presented alongside output as a measure of progress, while physical AI is designed to raise output per worker.

This analysis expects the two to coexist through a split: labor-intensive assembly, where this analysis sees India's cost advantage as strongest, keeping people at the center, and precision-critical or customer-specified processes automating. Whether policy begins to present productivity gains alongside job creation is an open question.

Implications for leadership

Leadership teams can consider four actions.

  1. Build the business case on quality, not headcount. Measure defect rates, rework, yield, and customer penalties avoided, which are more likely than labor savings to justify investment in India.

  2. Start where customers set the standard. Prioritize lines serving global brands and export programs.

  3. Treat humanoids as an option, not a plan. Track humanoid cost and uptime against McKinsey's benchmarks, and focus near-term investment on established forms of automation.

  4. Make the productivity case to policy makers. Firms that can show automation raising output and export competitiveness will be better placed as manufacturing policy evolves.

Indicators to monitor

Four indicators will show whether physical AI is spreading beyond a few industries in India: the share of installations outside automotive in the IFR's next India breakdown; humanoid unit costs relative to McKinsey's $20,000 to $50,000 range; whether India's installation growth keeps pace with its 27 percent five-year average; and how government reporting on manufacturing incentives treats productivity alongside employment.

Physical AI is unlikely to arrive in India the way it is arriving in higher-wage economies, as a substitute for expensive labor. This analysis expects it to arrive line by line, wherever quality, customer requirements, or safety make a case that wages alone cannot. Companies that wait for robots to become cheaper than labor may wait a long time. Those that justify them on quality are likely to move first.
