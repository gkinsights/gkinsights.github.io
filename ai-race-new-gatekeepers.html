Same budget, new gatekeepers: What the past month changed in the AI race

Between late October and mid-November 2026, the US midterm elections, a leadership crisis at Nvidia, and the sharpest one-day fall in the Nasdaq since April 2025 hit the AI industry at once. No large spender has cut its AI spending plan since. This analysis argues that they changed three other things: who decides where that money can be spent, which suppliers collect it, and how much it costs to raise.

by Gaurav Kumar · November 2026

For most of the past three years, the AI race has been described as a contest of chips and models, settled by whoever could buy the most computing power and train the best systems. The constraint was supply. The past month suggests that the constraint is moving. Local voters, congressional committees, and public market investors each acted on the industry within a few weeks of one another, and each now sits between the money and the computing capacity it is meant to buy.

The argument here is an interpretation of four weeks of events, filings, and reports, several of which are still developing. The indicators at the end of this article are the test of it.

The budget held, and it now exceeds the cash that funds it

The largest spenders raised or held their plans in their third-quarter results, released in the two weeks before the election. Alphabet's purchases of property and equipment rose to $51.4 billion in the quarter, from $24.0 billion a year earlier. Meta narrowed its 2026 capital expenditure outlook to $135 billion to $145 billion, from $130 billion to $145 billion. Amazon guided to about $220 billion of cash capital expenditures for 2026, and its chief executive projected AWS revenue growth of more than 40 percent in both 2027 and 2028.

Demand is still arriving to absorb that spending. Google Cloud revenue rose 103 percent to $30.7 billion in the third quarter, and AWS revenue rose 39 percent to $46.0 billion. At the model layer, Anthropic's amended IPO filing reports revenue of $17.0 billion for the third quarter of 2026 and committed spend of about $900.9 billion, substantially all of it for compute and infrastructure, of which about $70.7 billion falls due in the twelve months after September 30, 2026.

What has changed is how the spending is funded. Tracing where each dollar of revenue went in the third quarter shows that two of the largest spenders now invest more than their operations generate (Exhibit 2).

In this analysis's reading, that is the hinge between the spending story and the market story. When the largest companies funded AI from surplus cash, the price that investors put on their shares had little bearing on how much they could build. With free cash flow negative at Alphabet and Meta in the quarter, and Alphabet borrowing at scale, the buildout now depends in part on lenders and equity buyers. That gives the other three shocks of the month more reach than they would have had a year ago.

Permission: voters and local governments now gate where capacity can be built

The midterm elections were held on November 3. As of 7:49 a.m. Pacific time on November 13, the Associated Press count showed Democrats with 255 House seats won and Republicans with 169, with 11 seats not counted as won for either party; AP's results do not say why or which way those seats will go. In the Senate, as of 7:47 a.m. Pacific time the same day, AP's count showed Democrats with a total of 55 seats and Republicans with 44, with one seat not counted as won or held over for either party. These counts may have changed since. The counts carry a "majority reached" label for Democrats in the House and a "control reached" label for them in the Senate; those labels are worked out from AP's seat counts and are not calls by AP.

The more direct signal for the AI industry came further down the ballot. On November 3, voters decided 41 local measures on data centers in 10 states. According to an unofficial tally by Duane Morris Government Strategies, 40 limited data center development and one allowed it. Ohio held 21 of the year's measures, all decided against data centers. No statewide measure reached the ballot. Rising household electricity bills ran through the campaign, and the Ash Center at Harvard records several candidates proposing data center moratoriums, an end to data center tax incentives, and liability for harms caused by AI systems.

Markets moved within two days. The Nasdaq Composite rose about 1.4 percent the day after the election, according to Forbes, and fell 5.52 percent on November 5, closing in correction territory, at least 10 percent below its October 6 record. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell more than 12 percent and Nvidia about 9.3 percent. An eToro analyst quoted by Fox Business summarized the market's interpretation as "the AI infrastructure buildout will become slower and more expensive."

In this analysis's reading, the local vote count is the harder constraint. A data center that cannot win a zoning vote cannot be accelerated by a larger capital budget.

The industry's response points the same way. OpenAI's post-election infrastructure framework pledged to "pay our own way on energy and infrastructure," according to Al Jazeera. In this analysis's reading, that is a commitment to absorb costs that were previously shared with ratepayers and local governments, which raises the cost of each unit of capacity rather than reducing the number of units planned. A competing view, argued by a Forbes contributor on November 5, holds that a more predictable regulatory environment could support long-term growth by building public trust. Both readings can be true: rules may become clearer while each site becomes more expensive to win.

Supply: Nvidia's crisis shifted money among chip suppliers

On November 11, Nvidia's board accepted the resignation of Jensen Huang as president, chief executive, and director, effective immediately. The company's filing gives no reason. It named Colette Kress, the chief financial officer, as interim chief executive while the board searches for a successor, and appointed Harvey C. Jones as the company's first board chair. The resignation followed a New York Times report on October 29 that US intelligence believed a businesswoman at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based Nvidia customer, had been recruited to gather information on Nvidia for Beijing, and later reports about Huang's personal relationships, including with that businesswoman. China has rejected the espionage allegations. Nvidia's market value fell below $4 trillion after the announcement, according to Al Jazeera.

In the week to November 13, Nvidia's shares fell 11 percent while AMD rose 30 percent, Broadcom 24 percent, and TSMC 11 percent, according to Yahoo Finance, which linked the moves to analysts' view that Huang's ties with vendors had won Nvidia vital components ahead of rivals, and to large customers designing their own chips. In this analysis's reading, that week was mostly a reallocation within a sector that had already fallen sharply on November 5.

Anthropic's IPO filing points the same way from the buyer's side. Seven suppliers of chips, memory, and cloud capacity have agreed to buy $26.0 billion of nonvoting Class N shares at the IPO price: at the midpoint of the range, about $10.0 billion each from Google and Nvidia, $2.5 billion from AMD, $1.0 billion each from Broadcom, Micron, and Microsoft, and $500 million from TSMC. In this analysis's reading, when competing suppliers all take equity in the same customer, the largest buyers of compute have bargaining power, and the market for AI hardware is less of a single-vendor market than the past three years suggested.

Control: pressure to tighten export policy is rising from both parties

The Nvidia episode reopened the question of how advanced chips reach China. On November 12, Representative Richard Neal and Senator Ron Wyden, the senior Democrats on the House Ways and Means and Senate Finance committees, opened an inquiry into the administration's approval of Nvidia H200 sales to China, including why Megaspeed could keep buying chips, according to TechCrunch. Republican leaders are pressing as well. A Republican-led group drawn from the House Foreign Affairs Committee, the Select Committee on the Chinese Communist Party, and the Intelligence Committee demanded a closed-door interview, and Huang has agreed to appear, though arrangements were still being negotiated as of November 13. Senate Banking Committee Chairman Tim Scott requested testimony from Huang on December 3 and from Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick on December 10, according to TechCrunch and International Business Times. Lutnick has agreed to appear.

OpenAI has reportedly backed bills to tighten chip controls, according to Bloomberg, though the company has not stated this publicly. Other reports identify the bills as the Chip Security Act, which would require location verification on export-controlled processors, the AI Overwatch Act, which would expand congressional review of changes to chip export rules, and the MATCH Act, which would press allies to align controls on chipmaking equipment. The bills are being discussed for the fiscal 2027 defense authorization bill, whose conference text is typically finalized in late November. On the other side of the race, DeepSeek reportedly plans to use Huawei's Ascend 950 chips as the core training hardware for its next model, which could move their partnership from inference into full training in 2027, according to DIGITIMES, which cites no statement from either company.

In this analysis's reading, these developments push the US and Chinese AI industries further toward separate supply chains. That narrows the export market for US chip suppliers, and it tests whether Chinese developers can train frontier models at scale on domestic hardware.

Price: investors are paying less for AI growth

The private and public markets for AI companies repriced within the same weeks. Anthropic's amended filing sets a range of $70 to $78 per share for 825 million Class A shares to be listed on the Nasdaq. Yahoo Finance reports that this implies a fully diluted valuation of roughly $1.3 trillion to $1.45 trillion, and TechCrunch, citing CNBC, reports that investors had expected about $2 trillion roughly a month earlier. OpenAI has reportedly raised $11 billion of a round that could reach about $20 billion at a $1.25 trillion valuation, according to Bloomberg as cited by TechCrunch; in late September, Bloomberg reported a target of $30 billion at $1.4 trillion.

The cost of money is not falling to offset this. On October 28, the Federal Reserve held its target range at 3.75 to 4 percent, said that "inflation remains elevated," and recorded two dissents from members who preferred to raise rates by a quarter point. For companies whose AI programs are now partly funded by debt and equity, a lower valuation raises the cost of equity, and a steady policy rate brings no relief on debt.

Verdict
In this analysis's reading, the past month left the largest AI spending plans in place and added three gatekeepers between that spending and the capacity it buys: local voters who decide where data centers can be built, lawmakers of both parties who decide where chips can be sold, and investors who decide what the money costs. Competing explanation: the strongest alternative reading is that the selloff and the lower valuations are the first stage of a cut to the budget itself. With free cash flow negative at two of the largest spenders, investors who refuse to fund the gap could force capital spending down within a year, which would make the price shock the decisive one. One month of market moves cannot separate a repricing from the start of a retreat. Testable call: when Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta give their first outlooks for 2027 capital spending with their fourth-quarter 2026 results early next year, each will guide above its 2026 level. A flat or lower outlook from any of the three would show that the price shock has reached the budget and would weaken this analysis's reading.

What leadership teams could consider

For companies that buy, build, or sell AI capacity, four actions follow from this reading.

  1. Price permission into capacity plans. Treat local consent, power costs, and water use as gating items in site and cloud capacity plans, and expect providers that pay their own way on energy to pass part of that cost to customers.

  2. Plan compute on more than one supplier. The week of Nvidia's leadership change showed how quickly a single-vendor dependency can become a governance question. Qualifying a second source for training or inference capacity reduces that exposure.

  3. Separate valuation signals from demand signals. Cloud revenue grew 39 percent at AWS and 103 percent at Google Cloud in the third quarter, and the Nasdaq entered a correction a week later. Plans should rest on bookings, usage, and contracted spend rather than on share prices.

  4. Treat export compliance as a board-level risk. Companies with partners or capacity in Southeast Asia should review how they verify where advanced chips end up, since one of the bills now in defense bill talks would require location verification on export-controlled processors.

Indicators to monitor
Nvidia's third-quarter fiscal 2027 results on November 17, and any change in its supply or China commentary under interim leadership; the final pricing of Anthropic's IPO, expected around November 18, against its $70 to $78 range; the defense authorization conference text on chip export controls in late November; the requested hearings on December 3 and December 10; the three remaining local data center measures in December; state legislative action on data center tax incentives and utility rates when sessions open in 2027; and the 2027 capital spending outlooks from Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta.

The AI race has been framed as a question of who can spend the most. The past month suggests a different question for 2027: who can turn spending into running capacity fastest, given local consent, export rules, and the cost of capital. Companies that plan only for chips and models may find that the binding constraints are now outside their engineering teams.
