Paying for the platform: Four strategic risks in ServiceNow's AI expansion

ServiceNow reported steady growth, a 98 percent renewal rate, and a higher AI sales target in its third quarter of 2026. Its filings also show a company that spent $9.2 billion on acquisitions in nine months without disclosing growth excluding them, saw its GAAP operating margin nearly halve, and keeps seats at the base of a pricing model that AI agents could erode.

by Gaurav Kumar · October 2026

ServiceNow is one of the strongest franchises in enterprise software. In the quarter ended September 30, 2026, subscription revenue rose 21 percent to $3.988 billion and the number of customers paying more than $5 million a year rose to 680 from 553. Management raised its 2026 target for annual contract value from AI products to $1.7 billion, from $1 billion at the start of the year, and reported a 98 percent renewal rate on its earnings call.

This review does not dispute that performance. It examines what sits beneath it, using the company's results releases, its quarterly filing, and its earnings call. The argument is that ServiceNow's next phase is being bought and financed in ways that make its underlying momentum harder to judge, at the same moment its seat-based pricing faces pressure from the AI agents it sells. That is an interpretation, and the indicators at the end of this review are the test of it.

Risk 1: Growth is steady, but its sources are harder to see

Subscription revenue grew 20.5 percent in constant currency in the third quarter, exactly the rate of a year earlier. That rate now includes businesses that were not part of ServiceNow a year ago: Moveworks, acquired in December 2025, Veza, acquired in March 2026, and Armis, acquired in April. ServiceNow does not report growth excluding acquisitions, and its quarterly filing says it did not present pro forma results because the effect of the 2026 acquisitions was not material to consolidated results.

That is an accounting judgment, and it does not settle how much the acquisitions add to growth. When ServiceNow announced the Armis deal in December 2025, Armis had more than $340 million of annual recurring revenue, growing more than 50 percent a year. Against full-year subscription revenue guidance of about $15.8 billion, that alone is roughly 2 percent of the base, by this review's calculation. In this review's interpretation, an unchanged headline rate that now includes acquired revenue suggests that growth in the core business has slowed somewhat, though the company's disclosures do not allow the size of that slowdown to be measured.

The forward indicator points the same way. Current remaining performance obligation, contracted revenue due in the next 12 months, grew 21 percent in constant currency in the third quarter, and management guided to 18.5 percent for the fourth. On the earnings call, the chief financial officer attributed the gap to renewals pulled forward into the third quarter, a higher share of on-premises deals driven by public sector demand, and some backlog that is excluded from the measure but will still reach revenue.

Risk 2: A premium price for acquired growth

ServiceNow paid $9.167 billion in cash for business combinations in the first nine months of 2026. The largest deal, Armis, carried preliminary purchase consideration of about $7.6 billion, more than 20 times the annual recurring revenue Armis reported at announcement four months earlier, by this review's calculation. Armis was growing quickly, so the multiple on revenue at closing was somewhat lower. Veza added about $1.2 billion.

Goodwill rose from $3.578 billion at the end of 2025 to $10.012 billion at the end of September, including preliminary goodwill of $5.323 billion from Armis alone. ServiceNow said at announcement that it would fund Armis with cash and debt. Total debt rose from $1.491 billion to $7.524 billion, including $2.086 billion of short-term debt after net commercial paper issuance of about $2.0 billion in the year, and the company repurchased about $2.2 billion of its shares in the first nine months of the year.

ServiceNow is now a more leveraged company with a much larger goodwill balance, a combination that leaves less room for an acquisition that underdelivers.

Security is a logical adjacency for a company that already runs IT workflows, and the chief executive said on the call that its recent acquisitions were running well ahead of what the company had anticipated, though he gave no figures. The risk is not the strategy. It is that a large share of the capital now committed rests on acquired growth that outsiders cannot track separately.

Risk 3: The gap between reported and adjusted margins is widening

ServiceNow reported a non-GAAP operating margin of 32.5 percent, ahead of its own guidance. Its GAAP operating margin was 9.5 percent, as the company rounds it, down from 17 percent a year earlier. The difference is made up of disclosed costs that are real for shareholders even when they are excluded from the headline measure (Exhibit 2).

Stock-based compensation rose 29 percent year on year by this review's calculation, faster than revenue. Amortization of purchased intangibles is likely, in this review's view, to stay elevated while the Armis and Veza intangibles are amortized. The severance line reflects what the quarterly filing describes as a workforce optimization that uses AI-driven efficiencies to streamline certain functions. None of these items is unusual on its own. Together, they widened the gap between non-GAAP and GAAP operating income from $568 million to $953 million in a year, by this review's calculation.

Risk 4: Seat-based pricing meets its own AI

On the earnings call, the chief executive described a hybrid model: seat-based subscriptions for budget predictability, with consumption charges for AI usage on top. Other executives explained that customers draw down "assists" as they use AI features, and the chief executive said consumption could raise revenue per user by as much as five times once AI is in production.

The model works if consumption grows faster than seats shrink. ServiceNow's own experience shows why that is not assured. Management said total headcount had declined slightly, with sales operations roles down significantly and fewer support hires needed because of AI, even as quota-carrying sales headcount rose by double digits. In this review's view, customers may apply the same logic to the people who work inside ServiceNow every day. The chief financial officer also said customers are not asking for outcome-based pricing, while adding that the company would be ready if they did. In this review's view, that suggests the company is pricing for buyer preferences as they are today rather than for the point at which AI reduces the number of people a customer needs.

What would reduce these risks

ServiceNow's leadership could consider four actions.

  1. Disclose organic growth. Report subscription revenue and current remaining performance obligation growth excluding 2026 acquisitions for at least the next four quarters.

  2. Report acquired performance against the deal case. Show annual recurring revenue growth for the security business, including Armis, against what was assumed at purchase.

  3. Separate seat and consumption revenue. Disclose what share of subscription revenue comes from seats and from assists, and how seat counts are trending at renewal.

  4. Set a leverage and dilution path. State a target for total debt and for stock-based compensation as a share of revenue.

Indicators to monitor
Fourth-quarter growth in current remaining performance obligation against the 18.5 percent guidance; any disclosure of growth excluding acquisitions; the gap between GAAP and non-GAAP operating margin; and whether AI annual contract value reaches the raised $1.7 billion target for 2026.

ServiceNow is not a company in difficulty. It is a high-quality franchise that has chosen to buy its way into adjacent markets and to finance part of that expansion with debt while continuing to buy back stock. If the acquired businesses grow as planned and consumption revenue outpaces any decline in seats, the strategy will look prescient. If not, this review expects that today's steady growth rate will be seen as partly acquired, and its margins as partly adjusted.
