The AI apocalypse, so far: A valuation event, not yet a revenue event for the largest platforms

Investor concern that AI would erode demand for enterprise software drove a sharp selloff in software stocks in early 2026. Results from two of the largest enterprise software platforms, industry spending forecasts, and employer surveys suggest a slower story, and this analysis argues that the more immediate risk lies in how software is priced rather than whether it is bought.

by Gaurav Kumar · October 2026

In early 2026, software stocks fell sharply as investors concluded that AI could undermine the business model of software-as-a-service. If AI tools can execute tasks directly, the reasoning went, companies would need fewer software seats, fewer applications, and fewer people to operate them. A broader version of the same fear, an AI apocalypse for white-collar employment, has run alongside it.

The mechanism behind the fear is plausible. But the evidence available so far suggests that, for the largest platforms at least, the damage has shown up in valuations and expectations rather than in revenue. This analysis argues that the more precise risk is a gradual repricing of software from seats toward consumption and outcomes. That is an interpretation, and the indicators at the end of this article are the test of it.

Valuations fell sharply while the largest platforms kept growing

By April 9, 2026, the iShares Expanded Tech-Software Sector ETF, a widely used benchmark for software stocks, had fallen more than 27 percent for the year, and an index of software-as-a-service stocks was down close to 40 percent, according to Bloomberg reporting, which attributed the decline to investor concern that AI offerings would erode demand. In the quarters ending June and July 2026, two of the largest enterprise software platforms reported continued growth (Exhibit 1).

Two vendors are not the market, and both have used acquisitions to add growth. Salesforce does not disclose how much of its 14 percent growth in current remaining performance obligation, a leading indicator of revenue, comes from Informatica, so organic momentum is harder to read. A narrower index of software-as-a-service stocks fell further in the selloff, and smaller or narrower vendors may be more exposed than these platforms. Industry forecasts are consistent with continued spending, however: in July 2026, Gartner projected worldwide software spending of $1.468 trillion in 2026, up 15.5 percent, against total IT spending growth of 14.2 percent.

Employer surveys show fewer AI-driven job cuts than respondents expected

Survey evidence on employment shows a similar gap between expectation and outcome. In McKinsey's 2025 survey, 32 percent of respondents expected AI to reduce head count in their organizations over the following year. In the 2026 survey, 14 percent reported such reductions, and two-thirds reported little or no change in employment from AI over the prior year. These are separate annual samples rather than the same respondents tracked over time. Expectations remain elevated: 39 percent of 2026 respondents anticipate AI-driven head count reductions in the next year.

None of this means the effects will not come. It suggests they are arriving more slowly than the narrative implies, which matters for investment, pricing, and workforce decisions made on a twelve-month horizon.

The more immediate exposure is the pricing model

The selloff pointed to a genuine vulnerability. Much of enterprise software has historically been priced per user, in this analysis's reading of the market. If AI performs work that people used to do inside an application, the number of human users, and therefore seats, can fall even as the software does more work. Demand for the capability can rise while the billing unit shrinks.

In this analysis, that is a repricing problem rather than an extinction problem, and it is likely to play out over contract cycles rather than quarters. Its early signs would appear in seat counts at renewal and in net revenue retention before they appear in reported revenue, which is supported by contracts already signed. Salesforce's Agentforce annual recurring revenue grew more than 240 percent year on year, and ServiceNow's AI products have passed $1 billion in annual contract value, which suggests they are already shifting value capture toward agents, consumption, and outcomes.

Three positions appear most exposed

On this reading, exposure depends on what a product is and how it is billed.

Seat-priced applications with thin workflow depth. Tools whose value lies mainly in an interface over simple data are the easiest for AI to bypass.

Point solutions without proprietary data. AI can assemble functionality from multiple sources, leaving products without unique data or a system-of-record role with little to defend.

Labor-based services priced by effort. The same logic applies outside software. Wherever revenue is tied to hours or headcount, AI-driven productivity transfers value to the buyer unless pricing changes.

Systems of record, platforms that own critical workflows and data, and vendors that can price on consumption or outcomes appear better positioned, because AI tools still need authoritative systems to read from and act in.

Implications for leadership

Leadership teams can consider four actions.

  1. Separate valuation signals from demand signals. Base planning on bookings, renewals, and retention rather than on share prices or sentiment.

  2. Stress-test the billing unit. Model a hypothetical scenario in which customers' user counts fall 20 or 30 percent while usage rises, and decide in advance how pricing should respond.

  3. Shift value capture toward outcomes and consumption. Introduce agent-based, usage-based, or outcome-linked pricing before customers force the change at renewal.

  4. Plan workforce changes on evidence. The gap between expected and reported AI-driven head count reductions suggests that announced savings should be validated before they are banked.

Indicators to monitor

Four indicators will show whether the disruption moves from valuations into fundamentals: seat counts and net revenue retention disclosed by seat-priced vendors; the share of revenue from consumption-based and agent-based pricing; growth in current remaining performance obligation, which leads revenue, particularly organic growth excluding acquisitions; and the next round of employer surveys on realized, not expected, AI-driven head count changes.

The AI apocalypse has so far been told mainly through share prices. The fundamentals now available from the largest platforms describe something less dramatic and more demanding: software is still being bought, but the basis on which it is charged is starting to move. Companies that treat the selloff as a demand collapse risk cutting in the wrong places. Those that treat it as a pricing warning have time to act, though possibly less than current growth rates suggest.
