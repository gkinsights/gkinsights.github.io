Cut or cover: When AI's freed capacity is worth more as growth than as savings

Employers cited AI in 121,401 announced US job cuts through October 2026, more than twice the total for 2025. Most of those decisions treat the capacity AI frees as cost to remove. Where customers are still underserved, the same capacity is likely worth more as wider coverage, but the simplest version of that idea, more accounts for every seller, works only under conditions that many markets do not meet.

by Gaurav Kumar · November 2026

Consider a firm with 100 client partners, each responsible for one large account. Suppose AI takes over enough of the research, preparation, and administration that each partner could handle two. Leadership then has a choice. It can keep 100 accounts and shrink the partner team toward 50, or keep 100 partners and go after 200 accounts. Through 2026, public announcements have described the first choice far more often than the second.

This article argues that the second choice deserves more weight than it gets, and that it is harder than it looks. Seller time is rarely the only thing that limits revenue. Buyer attention, the quality of the next accounts, the roles in which capacity is freed, and delivery capacity often bind first. The argument is an interpretation of public evidence from 2025 and 2026, and the indicators near the end of the article are the test of it.

Announcements point to cuts, while executive surveys point to reinvestment

Challenger, Gray & Christmas, which tracks the reasons employers give for announced US job cuts, recorded AI as the most cited reason for several months in 2026. AI-cited cuts peaked at 38,579 in May, about 40 percent of that month's total, and fell to 1,265 in October, under 3 percent. The count for January to October, 121,401, compares with 54,836 for all of 2025. These figures record the reasons employers state, not verified causes.

Executive surveys describe a different pattern. In EY's US AI Pulse survey, fielded in September and October 2025, only 17 percent of organizations with AI-driven productivity gains said those gains had led to reduced headcount. More said they were expanding existing AI capabilities (47 percent), funding research and development (39 percent), or cutting prices to gain market share (29 percent). In EY-Parthenon's survey of 1,200 chief executives in August and September 2026, 48 percent said they were reinvesting the extra capacity in innovation and transformation, and 23 percent said they were struggling to turn productivity gains into measurable financial results.

The two pictures can both hold. Announcements are concentrated among large technology employers, surveys cover a broader base of firms, and executives have reasons to present AI as growth investment to survey takers and as efficiency to investors. In this article's reading, the more telling evidence comes from sales, where Gartner measured the capacity directly. AI tools save sellers an average of 4.8 hours a week, yet 72 percent of sales organizations report low reinvestment of that time in high-value selling. Organizations that do reinvest it are 2.2 times more likely to exceed their customer growth goals. In most sales teams, the capacity is created and then left unused, a weaker outcome than either cutting or covering.

Cutting is the default because it pays sooner and more certainly

A headcount reduction appears in the next quarter's cost base. Revenue from wider coverage arrives only after people are hired or moved, trained, and taken through a full sales cycle, and it may not arrive at all. For a leadership team that reports every quarter, that difference in timing and certainty explains much of the preference for cuts.

Markets have rewarded the preference. On February 26, 2026, Block said it would reduce its workforce from more than 10,000 people to just under 6,000 as it reorganized around AI, raised its 2026 guidance, and saw its shares rise more than 20 percent in after-hours trading. Its chief executive, Jack Dorsey, also said the company had over-hired during the pandemic, so the same announcement combined an AI rationale with an ordinary cost correction.

Some cuts fund capital spending rather than reflect freed capacity. Amazon removed about 14,000 corporate roles in October 2025 and about 16,000 more in January 2026, and has guided to about $220 billion of cash capital expenditure for 2026. Its chief executive, Andy Jassy, said the October cuts were driven by culture rather than by finances or AI. Oxford Economics, in a January 2026 briefing, found little evidence of AI replacing workers at scale and suggested that some firms present layoffs as a technology story rather than as a correction of past over-hiring. Forrester calls this AI-washing. Part of what looks like freed capacity being cut is ordinary cost reduction under a new label.

Measurement adds to the bias. A saving is a line in the budget. Hours returned to a seller or a partner stay invisible unless someone decides what they are for, which is the gap Gartner's 72 percent figure describes.

The case for spending freed capacity on growth

The economic case starts with demand. James Bessen's study of two centuries of US textile, steel, and automobile production found that automation raised employment in each industry for decades, because falling prices drew in large unmet demand, and reduced employment only once that demand was satisfied. In his words, "the elasticity of demand determines the sign of the change." Applied to a company, the question is whether customers would buy more if they were served more fully or more cheaply. Where they would, freed capacity has a productive use beyond the cost line.

The competitive case is that savings rarely stay with the firm that makes them. Warren Buffett's 1985 letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders explains why cost-cutting investments in its textile mills promised more than they could deliver: competitors were making the same investments, and "their reduced costs became the baseline for reduced prices" across the industry. AI tools are available to every competitor at similar cost, and in the EY survey 29 percent of firms were already using AI gains to cut prices for market share. A firm that banks its freed capacity as margin is likely to keep only part of it once rivals do the same. In this article's reading, the opening for coverage is largest where competitors are cutting client-facing roles, because their customers lose attention at the same moment.

The available evidence, though correlational, favors firms that aim AI at growth. In McKinsey's 2025 State of AI survey, 80 percent of respondents said their companies set efficiency as an AI objective, while the small group of high performers was more likely to set growth or innovation objectives as well. Only 39 percent of respondents reported any enterprise-level EBIT impact from AI. Gartner's finding on sales teams that reinvest saved time points the same way. Stronger firms may simply be better placed to pursue growth, so these results show association rather than cause. On valuation, McKinsey's 2014 study of software and online services companies found that, below $4 billion of revenue, faster growth produced about twice the market value gain of margin improvement.

Two large companies describe the pattern this article favors. IBM's chief executive, Arvind Krishna, said in May 2025 that AI agents had taken over the work of a few hundred human resources employees while IBM's total employment rose, because the savings funded more programmers and salespeople. Salesforce reduced its support headcount from about 9,000 to about 5,000 as AI agents took on about half of customer conversations. It said it had redeployed hundreds of employees into sales, professional services, and customer success, and Marc Benioff said agents now follow up on more than 100 million leads the company never had the people to call back. Both companies cut where the work was fixed and added where demand was unmet.

Cutting also carries a reversal cost that the first year's saving does not show. Klarna said in 2024 that its AI assistant was doing the work of 700 customer service agents. In May 2025 its chief executive said cost had weighed too heavily in that decision, that quality had suffered, and that the company would hire human agents again. Forrester reports that 55 percent of employers regret layoffs made because of AI and expects about half of AI-attributed layoffs to be reversed, often offshore or at lower pay. Gartner expects half of the organizations that planned significant customer service cuts because of AI to abandon those plans by 2027.

The same freed capacity can lower this year's costs or raise next year's revenue. Which is worth more depends on whether customers are waiting to be served.

Where the simple version breaks

Doubling the accounts per partner assumes that partner time is what limits revenue. In many markets, other constraints bind first (Exhibit 3).

The attention constraint is the easiest to miss. AI makes outreach and marketing content close to free, so every competitor can produce more of both, but buyers do not gain time. Gartner's 2026 buyer survey found that 67 percent of buyers prefer a buying process without a sales rep. Instantly, a cold email platform, reports an average reply rate of 3.43 percent across its users' 2025 campaigns. Gartner also predicts that by 2028 AI agents will intermediate 90 percent of B2B purchasing, worth more than $15 trillion, which would put software between sellers and buyers at the first screen. When every vendor doubles its reach, the likely result is more noise and stricter filtering on the buyer's side, with little gain in meetings for any single vendor.

The roles constraint follows from where AI acts first. The work it removes sits mostly in support and entry-level roles: Gartner found in November 2026 that 21 percent of service and support leaders had reduced human agent headcount because of AI, and Stanford researchers found a 13 percent relative decline in employment for workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations. A support agent or a junior analyst does not become a client partner in a quarter. Covering more accounts usually means hiring or developing different people, which is slower and costlier than the cut it replaces.

In professional and technology services, the thesis gains force and loses margin at the same time. When AI lowers the effort behind each outcome, clients expect prices to follow; McKinsey's scenario analysis, discussed in an earlier article on this site, puts the possible compression of core technology services revenue at 20 to 30 percent over time. A provider in that position needs more accounts just to hold revenue flat, so standing still on coverage is itself a decline. Software vendors that price by the seat face a related problem as their customers cut the staff who use those seats, a risk examined in the review of Salesforce's AI pivot. In both cases, the same number of accounts brings in less revenue than it used to, which raises the coverage a firm needs just to stand still.

A rule for choosing: find the constraint that binds

The choice between cutting and covering turns on two questions. The first is whether customers would buy more if they were served more fully or more cheaply. The second is whether the freed capacity sits in roles that face customers or in work behind them. Exhibit 4 sets out the four combinations.

Most companies will find different answers in different units. A firm can be right to cut its support center and wrong to cut its client partners in the same year.

A better version of the 100-to-200 idea

The strongest form of the thesis keeps the 100 partners and changes what surrounds them. Automation of back-office work funds growth roles instead of falling to margin. AI agents and digital channels cover the long tail of smaller accounts and unworked leads that never justified a partner's time, as Salesforce now does with its backlog of unanswered leads. Partners spend the hours AI returns at the points where buyers still want a person: checking what their own AI tools have told them, negotiating, and widening the relationship. The firm ends up covering far more than 200 accounts at different depths, rather than 200 accounts at half the depth each.

Indicators to monitor
AI-cited job cuts in Challenger's monthly reports, which fell from 38,579 in May to 1,265 in October 2026, as a sign of whether the first wave of AI-attributed cuts has passed; the share of sales organizations that reinvest AI time savings, against Gartner's 72 percent low-reinvestment reading; rehiring into roles cut because of AI, which Forrester expects; revenue growth alongside revenue per employee at companies that cut, which separates efficiency from shrinkage; and seller and client partner headcount at companies that reduce support and back-office roles, which shows whether the savings moved to growth.

What leadership teams could consider

Leadership teams could consider five actions.

  1. Identify the constraint that limits revenue before setting headcount targets. If demand is the limit, cutting is defensible; if coverage is the limit, cutting removes the capacity that growth requires.

  2. Set aside a fixed share of AI savings for growth and report it separately, with its own measures: hours reinvested, accounts covered, and conversion in newly covered segments.

  3. Assign breadth to AI and depth to people. Agents can handle early research and the long tail, while partner time goes to validation, negotiation, and expansion.

  4. Protect the route into senior client roles. Today's entry-level analysts and associates are the future pool of client partners, and that pool is the first to shrink.

  5. Count the cost of reversal in every cut. If Forrester's forecast holds, a share of AI-attributed roles will return, often offshore or at lower pay, after the knowledge has left.

Cutting and covering start from the same estimate of how much work AI removes. They differ on a second estimate, of whether customers would buy more if they were served better. Where the answer is yes, a company that books its freed capacity as margin is likely to watch competitors spend theirs on its customers. Where the answer is no, cutting is the sound choice, with the expectation that price competition will take part of the saving.
