Agents versus seats: Four strategic risks in Salesforce's AI pivot

Salesforce reported 11 percent revenue growth, sharply higher free cash flow, and fast-growing AI products in its latest quarter. Its public filings also support a review of four strategic risks: an AI product that can reduce the seats its applications are sold by, growth excluding its largest acquisition well below headline growth, a pricing model in transition, and a share buyback funded with new debt during its shift to AI agents.

by Gaurav Kumar · October 2026

Salesforce is one of the most successful enterprise software companies ever built, and by most financial measures it is performing well. In the quarter ended July 31, 2026, revenue rose 11 percent to $11.345 billion, non-GAAP operating margin was 34.1 percent, and free cash flow grew 81 percent. Annual recurring revenue for Agentforce, a metric the company broadened that quarter to include its AI offerings, Slackbot, and Headless 360, exceeded $1.5 billion.

This review does not dispute that performance. It examines the risks beneath it, using public filings, company statements, and press reports. The analysis argues that the central risk is structural rather than cyclical: Salesforce's most important new product is designed to do work that people would otherwise do inside its seat-priced applications. That is an interpretation, and the indicators at the end of this review are the test of it.

Risk 1: The new product's success can shrink the core billing unit

Many of Salesforce's applications are sold on a per-user basis. Agentforce is designed to handle work that people would otherwise do inside those applications. If it succeeds, customers may need fewer human users, and therefore fewer seats, even as they use Salesforce more.

Salesforce has shown what AI agents can do inside its own business. In late August 2025, its chief executive said on a podcast that the company had reduced its customer support workforce from about 9,000 to about 5,000, with AI agents handling roughly half of customer interactions. That is a strong proof point for Agentforce. In this review's view, it is also an illustration of what customers may do to their own staffing, and over time to the seats they license.

The company's reported categories show where that pressure would land. Agentforce Apps, which include the Sales, Service, Marketing, Commerce, and Slack franchises along with some AI consumption revenue, generated $7.19 billion of subscription and support revenue in the quarter, about two-thirds of that total, growing 8 percent in constant currency.

Risk 2: Growth excluding acquisitions is well below headline growth

Revenue grew 11 percent in the latest quarter. Excluding the $456 million contributed by Informatica, acquired in November 2025, growth was about 6.4 percent on a reported-currency basis, by this review's calculation. That compares with 10 percent reported growth in the same quarter a year earlier, before the acquisition. The calculation does not strip out smaller acquisitions, so true organic growth may be somewhat lower.

The pattern holds within the faster-growing category. Data 360, Headless Platform and Other grew 20 percent, but excluding $440 million of Informatica subscription revenue, it grew about 5.7 percent by this review's calculation. Salesforce has since completed the acquisitions of Contentful and Fin, which are included in its raised full-year guidance of 11 to 12 percent growth, alongside slightly over 3 points from Informatica.

Acquiring growth is a legitimate strategy. The point is that headline growth now says less about the momentum of the core franchises. In fiscal 2027, Salesforce also replaced its separate Sales, Service, and Marketing and Commerce figures with two broader categories. It restated the prior year, so year-on-year comparison remains possible, but cloud-level trends are no longer visible.

Risk 3: The pricing model is in transition

Salesforce now sells AI capacity in several ways at once. In May 2025 it introduced Flex Credits, priced at $0.10 per agent action, announced per-user Agentforce add-ons, and introduced a Flex Agreement that lets customers convert between user licenses and credits. In its latest results it also reports a usage measure, Agentic Work Units, which reached 3.2 billion in the quarter.

This flexibility is a sensible response to the seat question: it gives Salesforce a way to bill for work done rather than people licensed. The risk lies in the transition. Customers can convert seat spending into credits, consumption revenue is typically less predictable than subscriptions, and a hybrid model makes it harder for outsiders to judge how much AI revenue is new spending and how much is substituted from existing licenses.

Risk 4: A debt-funded buyback during the shift to AI agents

In March 2026, Salesforce began a $25 billion accelerated share repurchase, half of a $50 billion authorization, which management described as a sign of confidence. Its prospectus supplement for $25 billion of new notes, issued at the same time, stated that the company expected to use all of the net proceeds for the repurchase. Total debt rose from $14.4 billion at the end of January 2026 to $39.3 billion at the end of July.

Returning capital is not in itself a weakness, and Salesforce's cash generation is strong. The risk is optionality. Higher debt leaves less room to respond if the seat transition proves more costly than expected, or if another large acquisition becomes strategically necessary. Separately, goodwill of $59.25 billion from past acquisitions carries the risk of impairment charges if acquired businesses underperform.

What would reduce these risks

Salesforce's leadership could consider four actions.

  1. Disclose organic growth. Report revenue and current remaining performance obligation growth excluding acquisitions each quarter.

  2. Report seat and retention trends. Disclose net revenue retention and seat trends for Agentforce Apps, so investors can see whether AI is adding to or substituting for licenses.

  3. Separate new AI revenue from conversions. Show how much Agentforce revenue comes from new spending rather than from converted seat licenses, and keep the metric's definition stable.

  4. Set a leverage target. State the debt level the company intends to return to after the repurchase, and the conditions under which buybacks would slow.

Indicators to monitor

Four indicators will show which way these risks resolve: constant-currency growth in Agentforce Apps, against its current 8 percent; revenue growth excluding acquisitions; the share of Agentforce revenue from consumption pricing and license conversions; and total debt after the repurchase settles.

Salesforce is not a company in trouble. It is a profitable incumbent attempting one of the hardest moves in software: building the product that could shrink its own billing unit, while growth excluding acquisitions has slowed and its debt has risen to fund a buyback. If Agentforce and consumption pricing grow faster than seats decline, the company will have managed the transition well. If not, this review expects that today's headline growth will be seen as partly acquired, and its per-share gains as partly funded by debt.
