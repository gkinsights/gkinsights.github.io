The shrinking denominator: Testing Workday's growth plan against its results

Workday promised about 15 percent subscription growth and a 30 percent margin for fiscal 2027. Its guidance now sits above the margin target and below the growth target, and nearly half of its per-share earnings gain comes from a shrinking share count, funded partly with borrowed money.

by Gaurav Kumar · November 2026

Framework: Said, Did, Delivered

On November 24, 2026, Workday reported subscription revenue of $2.532 billion for the third quarter of fiscal 2027, the quarter ended October 31, up 12.8 percent. It raised full-year subscription revenue guidance to $9.958 billion and its non-GAAP operating margin guidance to 31.5 percent. It also guided fourth-quarter subscription growth to 10 percent, below the 12.8 percent it reported for the third quarter, which itself beat a guide of 12 percent.

The tension is that the company is ahead of its plan on one axis and behind it on another. At its Financial Analyst Day on September 17, 2024, Workday set a medium-term outlook of about 15 percent annual subscription revenue growth for fiscal 2026 and 2027, a 30 percent non-GAAP operating margin in fiscal 2027, and net dilution of about 1 percent a year. This review tests that plan against where capital went and what the filings show. The argument, an interpretation of public disclosures, is that the margin and per-share promises are being kept, while the growth promise is drifting, and that per-share results are being lifted by a shrinking share count while the growth gap stays open.

Said: the 2024 plan promised balanced growth, with margin and dilution as guardrails

Reduced to one testable sentence, the claim was that Workday would grow subscription revenue about 15 percent a year through fiscal 2027 while reaching a 30 percent non-GAAP operating margin in that year. The plan assumed acquisitions at recent levels with a non-material effect on near-term results, and it targeted net dilution of about 1 percent a year after buybacks. In February 2026, co-founder Aneel Bhusri returned as chief executive, with the company framing its next chapter around artificial intelligence; that release reaffirmed the fiscal 2026 outlook, apart from GAAP operating margin, but did not restate the medium-term targets.

Did: capital went mostly to retiring shares, funded in part by debt

Workday repurchased about $2.9 billion of stock in fiscal 2026 and $3.657 billion, or 25.6 million shares, in the first nine months of fiscal 2027. On a cash basis, the nine-month cash flow statement shows repurchases of $3,674 million against free cash flow of $1,767 million, about 2.1 times, by this review's calculation. Workday's cash flow is weighted to the fourth quarter, so the full-year ratio is likely to be lower; in fiscal 2026, repurchases were roughly equal to free cash flow of $2.777 billion. In October 2026, the company issued $2.5 billion of senior notes at rates of 5.700 to 6.500 percent, and total debt principal reached $5.5 billion. Cash, cash equivalents, and marketable securities ended the quarter at $5.643 billion, close to the $5.443 billion of January 31, 2026, which suggests the new notes in effect replaced the cash spent on buybacks.

The company also bought growth and cut cost. Business combinations used $2,079 million of cash in fiscal 2026, covering Paradox, Sana, and Pipedream, and a subsequent-event note discloses the purchase of ConductorOne (C1.ai) for about $850 million in November 2026. Restructuring plans in February 2025, February 2026, and September 2026 eliminated about 7.5 percent, 2 percent, and 2 percent of positions. Employees stood at 20,941 on October 31, 2026, against 20,588 a year earlier.

Delivered: margin beat the plan while growth fell short, even with acquisitions included

Fiscal 2026 subscription revenue grew 14.5 percent, close to the 15 percent target, and Workday's release states that its backlog figures that year include Paradox and Sana. Fiscal 2027 guidance of 13 percent compares a year with Paradox and Sana owned throughout against a year in which they were owned for only part, so acquired revenue lifts the rate by an amount the company does not disclose. The margin guidance of 31.5 percent is above the 30 percent target, against 29.6 percent delivered in fiscal 2026.

The more forward-looking signal sits in the backlog. Total subscription revenue backlog, the contracted revenue not yet recognized, grew 6.1 percent to $27.548 billion, against 17.0 percent a year earlier. The 12-month portion grew 12.3 percent to $9.216 billion, so the portion due beyond 12 months grew about 3 percent, to $18.33 billion from $17.75 billion, by this review's calculation. Part of the slowdown is a base effect: the year-earlier figure was lifted by Paradox, which closed in that quarter, and backlog is also seasonal, peaking at fiscal year end. One reading is that customers are committing for shorter terms or fewer workers; another is that renewal timing has pulled revenue into the near-term window. Either way, the long-dated book is growing far slower than the business.

The pricing model explains why this matters. Workday's 10-Q states that its subscription model is largely based on the size of customers' employee headcount, and that renewals at lower headcount could reduce revenue. Gross revenue retention was about 97 percent, so customers are staying. AI products contributed nearly $150 million of new annual contract value in the quarter, more than three times a year earlier, and more than 2,200 core customers have bought at least one AI product. These are early signs of a new revenue stream. Workday does not disclose AI revenue, so whether it can offset a slower per-worker base cannot yet be measured.

The receipt shows the trade-off in miniature. The average dollar still funds selling and product development at about $54 combined; the next dollar funds them at about $40, while operating income takes a share roughly double its average. That is the plan's margin promise being kept. In this review's reading, it also means the incremental dollar is going to margin rather than to selling capacity.

Workday is keeping its margin and per-share promises while drifting on the growth promise.

Verdict
Drifting. Said and did agree on margin and capital return, and both are being delivered. Said and delivered part on growth: about 15 percent was promised, 13 percent including acquisitions is guided, and the fourth-quarter guide of 10 percent points to a lower exit rate, though Workday beat its third-quarter guide. Did and delivered also part: about $2.9 billion of acquisitions and a CEO change centered on AI have not yet lifted growth, while $6.6 billion of buybacks supplied nearly half of the third quarter's non-GAAP earnings per share gain, by this review's calculation. Competing explanation: the slowdown may be cyclical and temporary, reflecting customers' cautious hiring under a headcount-based model and the timing of renewals, while new AI contract value more than tripling signals a new engine that has not yet reached revenue. Acquisition base effects also exaggerate the backlog slowdown. On that reading, growth stabilizes once AI products scale. The evidence cannot yet separate the two, because Workday does not disclose organic growth or AI revenue, but the fall in long-dated backlog growth to about 3 percent favors a slower base rather than a timing blip. Testable call: with its fourth-quarter fiscal 2027 results, for the fiscal year ending January 31, 2027, Workday will guide fiscal 2028 subscription revenue growth below 11 percent at the midpoint. Guidance of 11 percent or more would show the engine holding and weaken this reading.

What Workday's leadership could consider

Workday's leadership could consider three actions.

  1. Report organic growth and AI revenue. Subscription growth excluding acquisitions closed in the prior year, and recognized revenue from AI products, would let investors judge whether the new engine is replacing the per-worker base.

  2. Reset the medium-term outlook in full. The 2024 targets end in fiscal 2027. A new outlook that states growth, margin, and dilution together would show which promise leads when they conflict.

  3. Tie buybacks to free cash flow. Nine-month repurchases at about twice free cash flow, alongside new borrowing at 5.700 to 6.500 percent, lift per-share results but do not lift growth. A stated ceiling relative to full-year free cash flow would make the trade-off explicit.

Indicators to monitor
Fiscal 2028 subscription growth and margin guidance; total subscription revenue backlog growth against the 6.1 percent of the third quarter; any disclosure of organic growth or AI revenue; repurchases relative to free cash flow and the remaining $3.3 billion authorization; gross revenue retention against about 97 percent; and the revenue contribution from C1.ai once its accounting is complete.

Workday is not a company in distress. It retains about 97 percent of revenue, is guiding to a margin above its own target, and is signing new AI contracts at a rising pace. The risk lies in what the headline per-share numbers hide. A company can deliver faster earnings by shrinking its denominator for a while, but the numerator is the customer base, and its long-dated commitments are growing at about 3 percent. The next plan will show whether Workday intends to grow into its share count or keep shrinking it.
