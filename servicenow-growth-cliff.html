The growth cliff: Why ServiceNow's second doubling will be harder than its first

ServiceNow has told investors it will double subscription revenue again by 2030. The arithmetic of that target describes a company that expects to grow more slowly than at any point in the past five years. The engines behind its first doubling are close to exhausted, and the engines it needs next sit in markets where other companies set the terms.

by Gaurav Kumar · October 2026

ServiceNow expects about $15.8 billion of subscription revenue in 2026. At its Financial Analyst Day in May 2026, management set a target of more than $30 billion by 2030, with an upside case of $32 billion. Doubling a business of this size in four years is an ambitious goal. It is also, by this review's calculation, a plan for growth of about 17 percent a year from the 2026 base, against 28.5 percent in constant currency as recently as 2022.

This review does not argue that ServiceNow is in decline. It argues that the company is approaching a growth cliff: the point at which the sources of its past growth stop contributing and new sources must take over. The argument draws on the company's results releases, annual report, investor day, and earnings calls, and on research from Gartner and Bain. It follows a simple structure: how ServiceNow has been winning, how it is winning now, and what it must do to keep winning. An earlier review on this site examined the financial risks in ServiceNow's acquisitions and margins; this one examines the growth engine itself.

In arithmetic terms, ServiceNow's 2030 plan is a deceleration plan. The question is whether even that slower path is secure.

Been winning: one franchise, sold deeper into the same companies

ServiceNow built its business by becoming the system that runs IT service inside large enterprises, then selling more modules to the same buyers. In this review's assessment, that model has largely run its course on one dimension. The company's latest fact sheet states that about 90 percent of the Fortune 500 use ServiceNow. The annual report counted about 8,400 customers at the end of 2024 and about 8,700 at the end of 2025; the company's investor materials round the 2025 figure to about 8,800. Either way, the customer count grew by only about 4 to 5 percent, by this review's calculation, in a year when subscription revenue grew 21 percent.

The gap between those two numbers is the story of the last five years. By this review's reading, almost all of ServiceNow's growth now comes from selling more to companies that already pay it. That is a strength while expansion continues: the renewal rate was 98 percent in the third quarter of 2026, and customers paying more than $5 million a year rose to 680, up about 23 percent from 553 a year earlier. It also means growth depends on one lever being pulled ever harder.

The company's own cohort data shows how powerful that lever has been. In its third quarter investor presentation, ServiceNow illustrates that the 2011 customer cohort, which started with $100 of annual contract value, has grown to $3,730, net of any losses. In this review's view, the question for the next doubling is not whether that engine works, but how much further it can run in an installed base that already includes most of the largest companies in the world.

Now winning: more products and higher prices for the same customers

ServiceNow's current growth rests on two moves inside its installed base. The first is breadth. At its investor day, the company said 91 percent of net new annual contract value in 2025 came from deals that included five or more products, and on the third quarter call the chief financial officer said 19 of the 20 largest deals included six or more. Its customer relationship management business has passed $1.8 billion of annual contract value, and security and risk has passed $1 billion.

The second is price. Management said new AI bundles should lift prices by 20 to 30 percent on average, and that half of net new annual contract value has already moved to pricing that is not based on seats. AI annual contract value is targeted at $1.7 billion for 2026, raised from $1 billion at the start of the year.

The cliff: the forward indicator is already at the 2030 pace

Each of today's growth engines has a limit. The clearest sign that the limits are near sits in ServiceNow's own guidance. The company guided growth in current remaining performance obligation, contracted revenue due in the next 12 months, to 18.5 percent in constant currency for the fourth quarter of 2026. Its investor presentation states that Armis adds about 1.75 points to that figure. Excluding Armis, the measure points to growth of about 16.75 percent, by this review's calculation (Exhibit 3).

Management cites two technical headwinds. Deployment services bundled into some large AI deals are carved out of subscription revenue and, in some cases, out of this measure; the company puts the effect on fourth quarter subscription revenue growth at about half a point but gives no figure for this measure. Backlog from contracts that customers can terminate for convenience, inherited through acquisitions, is excluded, worth about half a point of backlog growth by year end. Treating each as worth a full half point of this measure, an assumption rather than a company figure, gives about 17.75 percent, by this review's calculation. In this review's view, either way, the organic forward indicator is already close to the roughly 17 percent annual pace that the 2030 target requires.

Wall 1: The largest buyers are already customers

With about 90 percent of the Fortune 500 on the platform and customer count growing in low single digits, new logos cannot carry growth at this scale. The remaining paths are smaller enterprises, which in this review's view are likely to buy less per customer, and markets outside North America. North America has supplied a steady 63 percent of trailing twelve-month revenue in each of the last five quarters, according to the investor presentation, which suggests, in this review's reading, that international markets are growing in line with the whole rather than ahead of it. Neither path, in this review's view, can replace the expansion engine. Growth therefore becomes a contest for a larger share of each existing customer's budget, which is harder to win every year.

Wall 2: A price increase is a step, not a slope

A 20 to 30 percent price lift on AI bundles adds growth while customers move onto the new bundles. Once most of the installed base has moved, in this review's interpretation, the higher price sits in the base and no longer adds to the growth rate. Unless consumption on top of those bundles compounds, this source of growth fades as the base completes its migration. ServiceNow's own executive brief on its AI strategy makes a related point: as AI models become cheaper, buyers will not pay lasting premiums for features any model can reproduce. Management's 2030 view, that AI will account for more than 30 percent of annual contract value, depends on that consumption compounding rather than on the one-time step.

Wall 3: The adjacencies belong to someone else

ServiceNow's fastest-growing businesses sit in markets where other companies own the system of record. Its annual report names Microsoft, Oracle, SAP, Salesforce, and Workday as competitors. In customer relationship management, the chief executive said on the third quarter call that customers are choosing to "rip and replace" incumbent systems rather than add ServiceNow alongside them. Rivals are pressing in the other direction. In February 2026, Salesforce said more than 180 organizations had selected Agentforce IT Service within about four months of its general availability, and named one customer replacing a legacy ServiceNow implementation.

These are vendor claims, and 180 organizations is small next to ServiceNow's base. The direction matters more than the number. In this review's view, ServiceNow is moving into its rivals' core markets while a large rival moves into ServiceNow's, which tends to raise the cost of every new dollar of growth on both sides.

In this review's view, the shift toward these contested markets is already visible in ServiceNow's sales mix (Exhibit 5). Over the year to September 2026, technology workflows, the IT core that now also includes Armis, fell to 48 percent of net new annual contract value from 52 percent a year earlier. Customer, industry, and core business workflows rose to 34 percent from 28 percent.

Wall 4: The layer ServiceNow sells is being contested

ServiceNow positions itself as the platform where work across systems gets done. In this review's view, AI agents compete for exactly that position. Bain's Technology Report 2025 describes a middle layer of agent operating systems, citing products from Microsoft, Google, and Amazon, that plan tasks and act across applications. In May 2026, Bain estimated that agentic AI could create a $100 billion US software market in cross-system coordination work, of which vendors capture only $4 billion to $6 billion today. Its examples of fast-scaling vendors were Glean and Sierra; it named no established workflow platform.

Gartner expects more than half of enterprises to drop paid assistive AI tools by 2028 in favor of platforms that guarantee workflow outcomes, and warns that software companies layering AI onto legacy applications could see margin compression of up to 80 percent by 2030. In this review's assessment, ServiceNow has a credible claim to the outcome layer, but not an uncontested one.

Future winning: the labor budget

ServiceNow's answer to these walls is to sell against labor, not software budgets. At its investor day, the chief financial officer described a support team of 20 analysts costing more than $1 million a year, about 90 percent of it labor. In the company's example, agents resolve 75 percent of the work and the customer's total cost falls by 65 percent, while the customer's spend with ServiceNow grows more than five times. The example works for ServiceNow because it starts from a small share of the customer's spend. It also shows the limit of the model. In this review's reading, most of the value released goes back to the customer, and ServiceNow's growth depends on repeating the conversion across many workflows and many functions, each with a different budget owner and a different set of rival vendors. That is a larger market than IT service. It is also a far more contested one.

The tension shows in management's own language. At the investor day, the chief financial officer said customers are paying for a resolved outcome rather than for tokens. On the third quarter call, she said customers are not asking at all for outcome-based pricing. Both can be true today. In this review's view, the gap between them is where the next pricing contest will be decided.

What would move the odds in ServiceNow's favor

In this review's assessment, four choices would make the second doubling more likely.

  1. Price for outcomes before buyers demand it. If Gartner's 2028 expectation holds, the vendor that sets outcome-based terms first will shape how the labor budget is split. Waiting for customers to ask cedes that design to competitors.

  2. Choose neutrality over conquest in adjacencies. ServiceNow's own executive brief says its AI Control Tower "only works if it's genuinely neutral." A sales motion built on replacing Salesforce or Workday sits uneasily with that claim. A control layer that works across those systems may be worth more than displacing them, and displacement invites retaliation in IT service, where ServiceNow has the most to lose.

  3. Disclose the growth mix. Report growth from new customers against expansion, and seat against non-seat annual contract value, so that investors can see when the price step ends and consumption takes over.

  4. Show labor-budget wins as a category. Report how much AI annual contract value is funded from business budgets outside IT. That is the clearest evidence that the next engine has started.

Indicators to monitor
Initial 2027 subscription revenue guidance against the roughly 17 percent annual path to $30 billion; fourth-quarter growth in current remaining performance obligation against the 18.5 percent guidance; customer count in the 2026 annual report; the technology workflows share of net new annual contract value; the share of net new annual contract value on pricing not based on seats; and further reports of customers replacing ServiceNow in IT service.

ServiceNow remains, in this review's view, one of the strongest franchises in enterprise software, and a 17 percent growth rate on a $16 billion base would be an exceptional outcome. The risk is not that the company stops growing. It is that the growth it needs next must be won in markets it does not control, against vendors and AI platforms competing for the same labor budget, at the same moment its traditional engines run out. If AI consumption compounds and the adjacencies hold, the 2030 target will look conservative. If not, this review expects the deceleration in Exhibit 1 to continue past the point management has planned for.
