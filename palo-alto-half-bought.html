Half bought: How much of Palo Alto Networks' 39 percent growth is its own

Palo Alto Networks reported 39 percent revenue growth and 66 percent growth in its headline recurring revenue metric for the quarter to October 2026. By this review's calculation from the company's disclosures, roughly half of the revenue pace was bought, and the part it earned is running at about 16 to 20 percent.

by Gaurav Kumar · November 2026

Framework: Earned, Bought, Borrowed

On November 18, 2026, Palo Alto Networks reported revenue of $3.45 billion for the first quarter of fiscal 2027, up 39 percent, and Next-Generation Security annual recurring revenue (NGS ARR) of $9.73 billion, up 66 percent. It raised its full-year revenue guidance to $14.52 billion to $14.57 billion, from $14.10 billion to $14.20 billion in September, and announced a $4.5 billion all-stock agreement to buy Applied Compute, a company that builds tools to post-train and run open-weight AI models.

The tension is that these headline rates now describe a different company from the one a year earlier. In fiscal 2026, Palo Alto Networks closed its purchase of Chronosphere, an observability company, on January 29, 2026, and of CyberArk, an identity security company, on February 11, 2026, for combined consideration of about $24.0 billion. This review separates the quarter's growth into what was earned, what was bought, and what was lifted by one-off or adjusted items. The argument, which is an interpretation of public filings, is that the earned engine is healthy at roughly 16 to 20 percent, and that the headline revenue rate is likely to converge toward it once the acquisitions lap in the spring.

Earned: the business Palo Alto Networks already owned is growing in the high teens

The company does not report organic growth, so the earned rate has to be read from two disclosures. The first is segment revenue. Network and AI Security, the firewall and secure access business, rose to $2,282 million from $1,904 million, 19.9 percent. Together with the corporate and other line, which grew to $163 million from $148 million, it grew 19.2 percent. The filing ties the CyberArk and Chronosphere revenue to the other two segments and names only organic product lines as this segment's growth drivers, though it does not state that the segment holds no acquired revenue; Koi Security, a smaller acquisition closed in April 2026, is not allocated.

The second is the pro forma disclosure the company gave while the deals were fresh. For the quarter ended April 30, 2026, the 10-Q showed combined revenue, as if Chronosphere and CyberArk had been owned throughout, of $3,043 million against $2,621 million a year earlier. That is growth of 16.1 percent, by this review's calculation, against reported growth of 31 percent. The two measures cover different quarters and scopes, so they bound the earned rate rather than pin it down. The first-quarter 10-Q presents no pro forma figures, stating that the effects of that quarter's two smaller acquisitions were not material.

Bought: acquisitions supplied a third or more of the revenue increase

Revenue rose by $971 million. Of that, $353 million is Idira, a segment the filing describes as identity security revenue from the CyberArk acquisition, which did not exist a year earlier. Cortex, the security operations business, added $225 million, growth the filing says came primarily from Chronosphere's observability revenue. The quarter also included Console Systems, added to Cortex, and Embrace Mobile, added to the observability platform, bought for $393 million and $292 million in September and August. Only the Network and AI Security and corporate lines, $393 million of the increase, can be read as largely earned.

Taken together, the two large deals were paid for mostly in stock. Of CyberArk's $21.1 billion purchase consideration, $18.5 billion was 112 million new shares and $2.3 billion was cash, while Chronosphere's $3.0 billion was almost entirely cash. Weighted diluted shares rose to 838 million from 709 million. On a per-share basis, revenue grew 17.8 percent, close to the earned rate above. The same applies to NGS ARR: its definition widens as the company adds acquired offerings, and the company guides full-year NGS ARR growth to 26 to 27 percent once the acquired base is in both periods, against 66 percent in the first quarter.

Borrowed: a one-off tax benefit, not operations, explains the rise in GAAP profit

GAAP net income rose to $482 million from $334 million, while GAAP operating income fell to $96 million from $309 million. The difference is a tax benefit of $457 million on pre-tax income of $25 million. Most of it, $369 million, is a discrete federal benefit on a fiscal 2018 tax position that, after recent court rulings on the 2017 tax law, the company now judges more likely than not to be sustained. Non-GAAP operating income of $1,059 million, a 30.7 percent margin, excludes $622 million of share-based compensation-related charges and $282 million of amortization of acquired intangibles, up from $39 million. Buybacks played no part: there were no repurchases in the quarter. The company also carries convertible notes assumed from CyberArk, with $1.1 billion of principal outstanding, whose change in fair value is excluded from non-GAAP results.

Verdict
Roughly half of the revenue growth story is earned. Taking the reported rate of 39 percent against an earned range of 16 to 20 percent, from the pro forma and segment evidence above, which come from different quarters, about half the headline pace comes from the businesses Palo Alto Networks already owned, and the rest from businesses it bought, mostly with stock. The borrowed share is small for revenue but large for GAAP profit, where a one-off tax benefit explains more than the whole rise in net income. Competing explanation: for a platform company, buying is the strategy, and acquired products sold into a large installed base become earned growth. On that reading, the bought share is a down payment on future earned growth, and the combined company should soon grow faster than the 16.1 percent pro forma pace it showed in the April 2026 quarter. With one quarter of pro forma data and none since, the evidence cannot yet separate the two readings. Testable call: at its fiscal third-quarter 2027 results (quarter ending April 30, 2027), the first quarter with CyberArk and Chronosphere in both periods for nearly the whole quarter, Palo Alto Networks will report total revenue growth below 20 percent and Network and AI Security segment growth below 20 percent. The company's raised full-year guidance already implies second-half revenue growth of about 18 percent, by this review's calculation; the segment call goes beyond it. Growth of 20 percent or more on either measure would show the platform lifting the earned rate and weaken this reading.

What Palo Alto Networks' leadership could consider

Palo Alto Networks' leadership could consider three actions.

  1. Report organic growth each quarter. Revenue and NGS ARR growth excluding acquisitions closed in the past year would let investors judge the earned engine directly, particularly as Applied Compute is added.

  2. Keep the pro forma series running. The pro forma disclosure given for the April 2026 quarter was the clearest view of the combined company; continuing it through the anniversary of the deals would show whether cross-selling is lifting growth.

  3. Show per-share progress alongside the 2030 target. Management has cited a target of $20 billion of NGS ARR by fiscal 2030. With share issuance now the main acquisition currency, reporting NGS ARR per diluted share would show whether stock-funded deals are adding value for each existing share.

Indicators to monitor
Network and AI Security segment growth against the 19.9 percent of the first quarter; Idira and Cortex growth once both deals lap; whether the company resumes pro forma or organic disclosure; diluted share count after the Applied Compute deal closes, expected in fiscal third-quarter 2027; and the gap between GAAP and non-GAAP operating income as amortization of acquired intangibles grows.

Palo Alto Networks is not a slow company dressed up as a fast one. An earned rate in the high teens at this scale is strong. The risk lies in reading the headline as the engine. By spring, the acquired revenue will sit in both periods, the reported rate is likely to fall toward what the business earns on its own, and the question will shift from how much the company can buy to how much its platform can sell.
