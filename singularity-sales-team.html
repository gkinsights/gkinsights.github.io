The singularity has a sales team

Machines are getting very good at a growing list of expensive tasks. The claim that the singularity has arrived rests less on that progress than on a spending bill that needs a story large enough to justify it.

by Gaurav Kumar · November 2026

The singularity has been announced again. This time the declaration came with a welcome message and nothing else: no test, no threshold and no date by which anyone could check it. A claim that cannot be measured cannot be wrong, and a claim that cannot be wrong is very useful to anyone selling something.

The word itself helps. In physics, a singularity is the point where the equations break and prediction stops. Borrowed for technology, it describes a moment when machines become so capable that the future can no longer be forecast. A moment like that has no clear edge by its own definition, so it can be declared at any time by anyone who finds the declaration convenient.

Something real is happening underneath the noise. Machines now write working software, draft contracts, summarize research and, this month, helped settle a mathematics problem that had stood for 167 years. Work that once took expert teams months now takes days.

Yet the same systems still need a person to check their work, and they still make mistakes a careful junior employee would catch. Capability arrives in sharp spikes, very strong in some places and weak in others. A real singularity would be general and self-sustaining, with machines improving machines and no human in the loop. What exists today is closer to a set of extraordinary tools that still need hands to hold them.

There is a simpler test than any benchmark. When a technology really arrives, nobody needs to announce it, because the prices announce it. Electricity did not need a press release; its arrival showed up in factory output, in the cost of light and in which businesses survived. If machines had crossed into general superintelligence, the evidence would sit in the accounts of the companies buying AI, with costs collapsing and output soaring on fewer people. Those numbers are moving, but slowly and unevenly, nothing like a break in history.

The reason for the announcements sits in the spending. The largest technology companies are on course to spend roughly $800 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026 alone, on chips, data centers and power. The extra revenue AI brings them is still a fraction of that, and estimates of the yearly gap run to hundreds of billions of dollars.

Spending at that scale needs a story at that scale. A better search engine does not justify a data center the size of a town. A smarter spreadsheet does not justify drawing as much electricity as a small country. Only one story is large enough to cover the bill: that intelligence itself is about to become cheap and unlimited, and that whoever owns it will own everything after. The singularity is that story.

Look at who needs the story to be true. AI labs raise money at valuations that assume the future has already arrived. Chip makers and power companies depend on order books that stay full only if the spending continues. Cloud companies have to explain to shareholders why spending keeps rising faster than sales. Investors need each funding round to be bigger than the last, and every one of these players benefits when the public believes the moment is now.

Urgency does particular work in a funding pitch. If the singularity is here, the cost of not investing becomes unlimited, because whoever falls behind may never catch up. That logic silences the ordinary questions about returns, payback periods and debt, and it turns a capital decision into a matter of survival. The same framing works on governments: an inevitable force looks pointless to regulate, and a race against a rival nation makes subsidies look patriotic.

History has seen this before. Railways in the 1840s and fiber-optic networks in the late 1990s were real technologies that changed the world, and both were sold on timelines far faster than reality delivered. Investors who bought the timeline lost fortunes. The tracks and cables stayed, and later businesses built empires on infrastructure they picked up cheaply from the wreckage.

That pattern is the most likely shape of what comes next. The technology is real and will keep improving, but the timeline being sold is faster than the economy can absorb, because companies, workers and laws change at human speed even when software does not. Somewhere between the announcement and the arrival, a great deal of money will change hands, and much of it will move from the people who believed the date to the people who sold it.

The honest reading is that the singularity is not here, and something important is. Machines have become very good at a growing list of expensive tasks, and that will reshape how companies spend and whom they hire. The declarations are a separate matter, best read the way an investor reads a pitch deck: as a statement of what the speaker needs to be true.
