The GCC divide: Why not every capability center will make the climb

India's 2,117 global capability centers employ an estimated 2.36 million people, and the industry's own data places 56 percent of them below its top two maturity stages. NASSCOM and Zinnov project that about three-quarters could reach those stages by FY2030. This analysis argues that the climb will be harder than that projection implies for centers whose work is most exposed to AI.

by Gaurav Kumar · October 2026

Few stories in Indian business have been told as consistently upbeat as the rise of global capability centers (GCCs), the in-house technology, operations, and innovation centers that multinational companies run in India. Industry research describes a steady climb up the value chain, from back offices to centers that own products, platforms, and increasingly AI capabilities.

That climb is real for many centers. The question is whether it is available to all of them. This analysis argues that outcomes are diverging, that the mandate a center holds shapes how far it can climb, and that AI is likely to widen the gap between centers that own decisions and centers that execute defined work. Those are interpretations, tested against the indicators at the end of this article.

The sector is large, and maturity is uneven

The NASSCOM and Zinnov India GCC Landscape Report, published in May 2026, counts 2,117 GCCs operating 3,728 units, with an estimated 2.36 million employees and estimated revenue of $98.4 billion in FY26. It classifies centers into four maturity stages, from Outposts focused on cost arbitrage to Transformation Hubs that lead business outcomes (Exhibit 1).

Satellites are not failing centers. Many deliver well against what they were set up to do. But the distribution shows that a majority of centers do not yet own outcomes end to end. The report also projects that about 75 percent of India's GCCs can reach Portfolio or Transformation status by FY2030, a forecast rather than an observed result.

Early signs of strain sit alongside the growth

Alongside this growth, Business Standard reported in July 2026 that data from Neovay showed more than 25 GCCs closing in 2025, and that research by Embark and UnearthIQ found about 30 percent of centers set up since 2021, roughly 150 centers, had plateaued. These estimates come from smaller firms and are not a comprehensive census, and 25 closures is a small number against a base of more than 2,100. They are best read as a signal that the sector's growth is not uniform.

Mandate shapes how far a center can climb

The NASSCOM and Zinnov stages describe an evolutionary path, and the report shows the share of centers at the top two stages rising since FY2021. This analysis adds a lens on why some move faster than others: the mandate a center holds. Three archetypes, which are this article's framework rather than the report's, are useful.

Arbitrage centers exist to perform defined work at lower cost. Their value to the parent is the cost gap, and their leaders typically execute rather than decide. When the cost gap narrows, or the work can be automated, their rationale weakens.

Capability centers exist to access talent the parent cannot hire at home in sufficient numbers. They are more durable, but their value depends on the parent continuing to route work to them.

Mandate centers own a product, platform, process, or budget, and their leaders hold decision rights. NASSCOM and Zinnov report that 64 percent of site leaders now hold a dual mandate that combines global functional ownership with site leadership, which suggests that decision rights are spreading across the sector.

EY's 2021 analysis of why some GCCs fail to deliver value partly echoes this lens. It points to weak executive sponsorship, fragmented process ownership, and site leaders without authority over budgets and resources. In this analysis, those are features of how a center is set up, which is why a center's mandate matters as much as its execution.

AI is likely to widen the gap

Experts quoted by Business Standard estimate that 1.1 million to 1.3 million GCC employees are engaged in repeatable work built on tickets, documents, and reports. That is the type of work generative AI is widely expected to compress, and in this analysis it is concentrated in centers closer to the arbitrage end of the spectrum.

For centers that own decisions, AI is an amplifier: they choose where to deploy it, and NASSCOM and Zinnov report that more than 1,200 GCCs already have AI and machine learning capabilities. For centers whose value lies in repeatable work, AI can substitute for the activity that justifies them. This analysis expects aggregate GCC revenue to keep growing while some sub-scale centers close, consolidate, or move to service providers, which would make the 2030 maturity projection harder to reach for the lower stages.

Implications for leadership

Leadership teams can consider four actions.

  1. Name the archetype honestly. Decide whether a center exists for cost, talent, or mandate, and set targets and investment accordingly rather than expecting every center to follow the same path.

  2. Grant decision rights deliberately. If the ambition is a Portfolio or Transformation Hub, transfer ownership of a product, process, or budget, and give the site leader a global role.

  3. Assess AI exposure by role. Map the share of a center's work that is repeatable and ticket-based, and decide whether to automate, redeploy, or restructure before economics force the decision.

  4. Treat sub-scale centers as portfolio decisions. Consolidation, transfer to a service provider, or exit can be better outcomes than a slow plateau.

Indicators to monitor

Four indicators will show whether the divide is widening: the share of centers at the Outpost and Satellite stages in the next NASSCOM and Zinnov report, against the projection of about 75 percent at higher stages by FY2030; the net number of new GCCs compared with the pace of the last five years; reported closures and consolidations; and the share of GCC roles in repeatable operations versus product and engineering.

India's GCC story is real, and for a large group of centers it is still being written. But the industry's projection assumes a climb that AI may make steeper for centers built around repeatable work. Not every center will make that climb, and not every center needs to. Parent companies that recognize which kind of center they run, and act on it, are likely to make better decisions than those expecting every center to follow the same ladder.
