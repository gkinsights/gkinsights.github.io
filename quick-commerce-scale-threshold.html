The scale threshold: Why India's quick commerce boom may not lift all platforms

A new industry forecast expects India's quick commerce market to grow five to seven times by 2031. The latest results of the two listed platforms suggest that the forecast says little about who will earn from that growth: on Swiggy's own guidance, Instamart needs more than twice its current scale to break even, while Blinkit, about three times larger, already earns a profit.

by Gaurav Kumar · October 2026

In October 2026, Redseer, in a report developed with Google, estimated India's quick commerce market at about $13 billion in fiscal 2026 and projected $70 billion to $90 billion by fiscal 2031, with about $90 billion as its headline figure, implying growth of 41 to 48 percent a year. That would lift the channel from about 1 percent of Indian retail to 4 to 5 percent. The firm's emphasis is on how the market grows: metro shoppers moving from top-up orders to monthly baskets, and smaller cities overcoming small baskets and trust concerns.

About two weeks later, the two listed platforms reported results for the quarter ended September 30, 2026. Eternal's Blinkit reported an adjusted EBITDA profit of ₹184 crore, against a loss a year earlier. Swiggy's Instamart reported an adjusted EBITDA loss of ₹895 crore, wider in rupees than a year earlier though narrower as a share of gross order value. This article argues that the gap between them is a gap of scale rather than of market, and that rules on foreign ownership shape who can close it. That is an interpretation of one quarter of results and company guidance; the indicators at the end are the test of it.

Swiggy's own guidance puts break-even at more than twice Instamart's current scale

The most useful number in the quarter came from the company that is losing money. Swiggy told shareholders that Instamart needs about ₹60,000 crore of annualized net order value, and about ₹30 of contribution per order, to reach adjusted EBITDA break-even. Its current annualized net order value is about ₹26,400 crore, so the threshold is roughly 2.3 times today's scale. Management expects about two-thirds of the needed improvement to come from higher revenue per order, through product mix, own brands, and advertising, and one-third from lower cost per order.

Blinkit is already past that line. Its quarterly net order value of ₹20,940 crore annualizes to about ₹83,800 crore, by this article's calculation, and it earned an adjusted EBITDA margin of 0.9 percent of net order value. Eternal reports that cities contributing about 70 percent of Blinkit's net order value are adjusted EBITDA positive, and that mature cities run above 5 percent. In this article's reading, the two companies are describing the same cost curve from opposite ends.

The forecast describes how big the market can become. The two platforms' results describe how big a player must become before the market pays it.

Owning inventory adds margin, but only for Indian-owned platforms

Scale is not the only lever. Both platforms are moving from a pure marketplace, in which sellers own the goods, toward owning inventory. A year ago, Eternal said about 80 percent of Blinkit's net order value came from owned inventory and that the shift should add about 1 percentage point of net margin over four to six quarters. Swiggy now plans the same move: what it calls its IOCC (Indian-owned and controlled company) transition, which its new cap on foreign ownership enables, starts in the middle of the December 2026 quarter and should complete over the following two to three quarters, which management says could add about 80 basis points to Instamart's contribution margin when fully rolled out.

The structure matters because India's foreign investment policy does not permit foreign investment in the inventory-based model of e-commerce; it permits up to 100 percent under the automatic route only for the marketplace model. In April 2025, Eternal proposed a 49.5 percent cap on foreign ownership, stating that it qualified as Indian-owned and controlled. Swiggy's shareholders approved the same cap in August 2026. A July 2026 policy change allows foreign-invested platforms to hold inventory only for exports, and a government release in August 2026 states that such inventory cannot be diverted to the domestic market.

In this article's interpretation, this creates an asymmetry. Indian-owned platforms can capture a retail margin on the goods they sell, while foreign-invested e-commerce entities competing in fast delivery must earn their return as a marketplace. Swiggy notes that horizontal e-commerce majors are competing actively through the festive quarter and that it does not expect competitive intensity to ease in the near term.

The strongest counterargument is timing, and Swiggy's own numbers weaken it

The most credible alternative reading is that Instamart's losses reflect a choice to invest through a competitive festive quarter rather than a structural disadvantage. Swiggy says about 100 basis points of pricing investment went into habit formation and winning back dormant users, which it expects to reverse, and it believes adjusted EBITDA losses peaked in this quarter. It guides contribution margin to between zero and minus 100 basis points in the December quarter, with adjusted EBITDA margin improving by about 100 basis points. Instamart's contribution margin already improved 157 basis points from a year earlier, even as the rupee loss grew.

Both readings can hold at once. Even if the pricing investment reverses and the inventory benefit arrives in full, those two levers amount to under 2 percentage points on management's figures, which are not necessarily measured on the same base, against an adjusted EBITDA loss of 9.7 percent of gross order value. The balance must come from the other levers Swiggy names, higher revenue per order through mix, own brands, and advertising, and lower costs per order, and Swiggy's own break-even framing pairs them with about 2.3 times today's volume. Blinkit's growth is slowing too, from net order value growth of 137 percent a year earlier to 79.3 percent, but that is still more than twice Instamart's 34.8 percent. In this article's view, a leader growing faster from a larger base makes the gap in absolute scale wider, not narrower.

What this means for brands and platforms

Leadership teams can consider four actions.

  1. Plan for a concentrated channel. Consumer brands building quick commerce distribution should assume that terms will be set by the platforms that have crossed the scale threshold, and budget for advertising as part of the cost of being stocked, since Swiggy names advertising as one of three sources of higher revenue per order.

  2. Concentrate before expanding. Swiggy frames break-even as a volume threshold. In this article's view, challengers that build density in fewer cities may reach it sooner than those pursuing national coverage.

  3. Treat ownership structure as strategy. Platforms and investors should price the inventory margin available only to Indian-owned and controlled companies into any comparison of competitors.

  4. Prepare for the January 2027 pricing rules. Discount-led customer acquisition will be harder to present once prior prices must reflect the lowest price of the past 30 days.

Indicators to monitor
Instamart's adjusted EBITDA margin against Swiggy's guided improvement of about 100 basis points in the December 2026 quarter; progress and margin effect of Instamart's move to an inventory model over the next two to three quarters; Blinkit's adjusted EBITDA margin as it adds stores toward 3,500 by March 2027; and any change in foreign investment rules for domestic inventory-based e-commerce. This article's call: when Swiggy reports results for the quarter ending March 31, 2027 (Q4 FY27), Instamart's adjusted EBITDA loss will still be wider than 7 percent of gross order value, roughly the path its current guidance implies. A loss narrower than 7 percent would show that the pricing reversal and the inventory model are closing the gap faster than scale alone, and that scale matters less than this article argues.

The forecast of a market of up to $90 billion by 2031 may well prove right. The quarter's results suggest it answers a different question from the one that decides returns. On one quarter of the listed platforms' own disclosures, the market rewards the player that has already reached the scale at which stores, inventory, and advertising pay for themselves, and the rules on ownership decide who may use one of those levers. For everyone else, the growth of the market is a necessary condition for profit, not a sufficient one.
