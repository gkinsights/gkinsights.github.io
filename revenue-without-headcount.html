Revenue without headcount: Rethinking the Indian IT services model

India's two largest IT services providers signed large deals in FY26 while the five largest together reduced headcount, and TCS expanded its margin. Leaders who treat this as a cyclical dip risk missing a structural shift in how the industry creates value.

by Gaurav Kumar · October 2026

For more than three decades, the Indian IT services industry has grown on a simple premise: revenue scales with people. Providers hired at volume, deployed talent against client demand, and billed for effort. That premise built an industry that employed an estimated 5.8 million people in FY25, according to NASSCOM, and anchors India's services exports.

Results for FY26 suggest the premise is weakening. The five largest providers ended the year with fewer employees than they started, even as the two largest reported strong deal wins and TCS widened its operating margin. Part of this reflects a soft demand environment. This analysis argues that a larger part reflects a change in the unit of work itself, driven by AI-enabled delivery. That is an interpretation rather than a settled finding, and the indicators at the end of this article are the test of it. The implications for pricing, organizational structure, and competitive position are significant, and they are likely to persist after demand recovers.

The relationship between revenue and headcount is weakening

In FY26, the five largest Indian IT services providers reported a combined net reduction of roughly 7,000 employees (published tallies range from 6,981 to 7,389), after net additions in FY25. TCS accounted for most of the decline: its reported headcount fell by 23,460, from 607,979 to 584,519. Over the same period, the two largest providers delivered outcomes that the traditional model would not predict (Exhibit 1).

Infosys grew revenue while reducing headcount in the final quarter. TCS expanded operating margin by 70 basis points while both revenue and headcount declined. Campus intake points in the same direction: TCS has reported about 25,000 fresher offers for FY27, compared with 40,000 or more in prior years, and has said further hiring depends on demand.

Cyclical pressure explains part of the shift, but not all of it

Discretionary technology spending in North America and Europe has been subdued, and periods of weak demand typically flatter productivity ratios. If the decline were purely cyclical, headcount would be expected to recover alongside demand, as it has before: after the pandemic slowdown, TCS alone added a net 103,546 employees in FY22.

There are reasons to expect a different pattern this time. The activities that formed the base of the delivery pyramid, including code generation, testing, documentation, first-level support, and data operations, are precisely those that AI tools are now compressing. If that compression holds, the same scope of work requires smaller teams, and clients have every incentive to price renewals accordingly. TCS reports annualized AI revenue above $2.3 billion, an indication that AI-enabled delivery is already embedded in commercial terms rather than confined to pilots. McKinsey, in a scenario analysis of agentic AI, estimates that core tech services revenue could compress by 20 to 30 percent over time, partly offset by new demand for AI implementation and orchestration. That is a projection, not an observed decline.

Three elements of the traditional model are under pressure

The commercial model. Time-and-materials pricing rewards effort. As effort per outcome declines, providers face a choice: pass productivity gains to clients and accept smaller contracts, or move to outcome-based pricing and assume delivery risk they have historically avoided. Deferring that choice is unlikely to remain viable as procurement teams become more informed about AI-driven productivity.

The organizational pyramid. A broad base of junior practitioners supporting a smaller senior layer has long been a core margin lever. As the base contracts, providers carry a proportionally heavier middle layer that is more costly and less leveraged. Pressure is therefore likely to extend beyond fresher intake to mid-level roles over the next two to three years.

The investment case. Valuations have historically rested on predictable volume growth. A model in which growth comes primarily from pricing, mix, and intellectual property is a different business, with different risk characteristics, and is likely to be valued differently.

Three competitive positions appear defensible

Platform and IP ownership. Providers that own reusable platforms or accelerators can retain a larger share of AI-driven productivity gains as margin, rather than passing them to clients as price reductions.

Proximity to client decisions. Providers embedded in advisory-led transformation, deep regulated-industry expertise, or operating responsibility for business outcomes are less exposed, because influence over decisions is harder to automate than delivery of tasks.

Specialist challengers. Mid-sized providers with focused capabilities may be able to adopt outcome-based pricing faster than large incumbents can restructure their contracts, and capture share in the most commoditized segments of incumbent portfolios.

The position most at risk is the one much of the industry still occupies: generalist delivery at scale, priced by effort.

Implications for provider leadership

Leadership teams can consider four actions.

  1. Measure the shift directly. Track revenue per employee and effort per outcome by service line, rather than relying on aggregate utilization, to separate cyclical effects from structural ones.

  2. Reprice deliberately. Identify the service lines where AI-driven productivity is highest and move them to outcome-based or fixed-price structures before clients force renegotiation on their terms.

  3. Redesign the talent model. Plan for a narrower base and a more leveraged middle layer, with reskilling targeted at domain expertise, solution design, and AI orchestration.

  4. Invest in owned assets. Shift investment from bench capacity toward platforms, accelerators, and industry-specific IP that allow productivity gains to be retained.

Indicators to monitor

Four indicators are likely to distinguish structural change from cyclical noise over the next several quarters: revenue per employee relative to headcount growth; the share of fixed-price and outcome-based contracts in new deal value; mid-level hiring and lateral movement, which is less visible than fresher hiring but more consequential for cost; and the continued expansion of global capability centers in India, which narrows the pool of work that enterprises choose to outsource.

The Indian IT services industry is not in structural decline. It is, however, approaching a choice between two models: a productivity utility with shrinking revenue per engagement, or a value-owning partner that prices on outcomes and retains the benefits of its own efficiency. Providers that make this choice early are likely to shape the terms on which the industry competes. Those that defer it may find that their clients' procurement functions make the choice for them.
